Don’t Panic.Regulation is Cool.

On what happens when AI reaches compliance, and who sees it first

To whom it may concern,

Ask a bank whether it is compliant, and time how long the answer takes.

It is a fair question. The bank holds your money under rules written for exactly that purpose. But the honest answer is not a number on a screen; it is a project. Someone has to find which rules apply, which policies cover them, which controls run, and where the evidence is, and most of that lives in spreadsheets and in the memory of the people who built them. I spent ten years building the technology that was supposed to make this easy, as a vendor and then inside one of the world’s largest trading platforms, and in all that time I never saw a firm that could answer the question the day it was asked.

The bill for that is enormous. The United States now employs 417,070 compliance officers, three times as many as in 2000. The standing body of US federal rules runs to 194,395 pages. At the twenty largest financial firms in the UK, compliance takes over 13% of operating costs, and the smallest banks in America spend a larger share of their payroll on it than the largest. My own estimate, from those figures and the roughly $6.5 trillion that banks, insurers, asset managers and payment firms spend running themselves each year, is that the world’s financial institutions spend about a trillion dollars a year on compliance: people, technology and advisers together. More than $90 billion of investment went into “regtech” in the last twelve years, and not one of those lines bent.

Here is what I think happens next. In 2027, the first regulated firms will hold a complete map of what they owe, drawn by machines from the rulebooks themselves and signed by the people responsible. In 2028, those firms will open a new country by changing a profile rather than hiring a department, and the compliance teams that took a decade to build will start to be rebuilt around the system instead of the spreadsheet. Most of what compliance officers do today, reading, mapping, checking, reporting, explaining, will be done by software that reads every rulebook in every language at once. The people will keep the judgment and the signature. Everything in between goes.

Almost nobody in the industry believes this yet. Compliance is judgment, they say; regulators will never accept it; we tried regtech, and look. I understand the reflex. I have bought the tools, built the integrations and lived with the gaps between them, and I would have said the same five years ago.

But some of us have seen it coming. We are the ones who built the surveillance stacks and the reporting pipelines at the big platforms, who have read the same duty in the FCA Handbook, the FINRA rulebook and MiFID and know it is one duty wearing three outfits. I came to it from regulatory reporting at a data provider, then spent seven years helping build the compliance technology function at a global broker from nothing. I am not a lawyer. I am not a compliance officer by title. What I have is a decade of watching what the tools could and could not do, and a long-standing LinkedIn slogan, Regulation is Cool, that got the laughs it deserved. Then, in 2023, machines went from failing the bar exam to passing it within a few months, and the price of reading a rulebook fell to cents. Perhaps we are wrong about the next two years. Perhaps we are the people who saw it first.

Let me tell you what we see.

Compliance has a vocabulary problem, not a tooling problem. Every rulebook, policy, system and team describes the same duties in different words, so the connections between them have to be held in people’s heads, and the heads keep leaving. There is a routine in arithmetic in which any four-digit number, put through one fixed procedure, arrives at 6174. Compliance should work like that: describe a company, run the procedure, get the program, every step traceable to its source. It does not, because nobody has written the procedure down.

So we are writing it down. CLHEAR is an open standard for describing a compliance program in one language: the exact words of the rules, the duties inside them, the roles, documents, systems and processes each duty requires, and the profile of the firm that owes them. The vision is a public reference that any firm, regulator or software maker can read: describe an organisation, and see the program it needs, with every line traced back to the clause it came from. It will be open because a language only one company can read is not a language, and because a standard earns trust only when the people outside the company that wrote it can test it and break it.

On top of the standard, Reg42 is building the system that runs the program: it maps what the rules require, compares that with what the firm actually does, and closes the gaps, with software doing the work and accountable people signing off. One intelligent system for the entire compliance program, in the firm’s own cloud, for financial services first and every regulated industry after. Our targets, and they are targets, not results: cut a firm’s total compliance cost by 80%, know its compliance score at any moment, and answer any compliance question in sixty seconds. The vision past the targets is a compliance program that runs itself and can prove it.

In the book our name comes from, a civilisation builds a computer to find the answer to everything, waits seven and a half million years, and gets 42, at which point it realises nobody wrote down the question. Compliance is the reverse. The answers are published daily, in a gazette that has not had a year under sixty thousand pages this century. What no firm has ever written down is the question: what, exactly, do we owe? That is the whole product.

Why now, and not in any of the regtech waves before? Because the work between the rulebook and the control is reading and writing, and reading and writing is the one thing machines learned to do in 2023. Not judgment. Not responsibility. The translation. Take the translation out of the hands of 417,070 people, and compliance becomes something a company has rather than something it does.

I could be wrong about the dates. I have watched enough roadmaps to know which way they slip. What I do not think I am wrong about is the direction, and the evidence for that is in the six pieces below, each written to stand alone, each with its sources and its charts. Part VI is where I argue against myself; start there if you are a sceptic. I hope you are.

  1. IFrom Tasks to the MapThe US employs 417,070 compliance officers, three times the 2000 count, and more than $90 billion of regtech investment did not bend the line. The tools automated tasks; the map of what a firm owes stayed in people’s heads. Since 2023 machines read rulebooks at a price that fell 200-fold, and a keyword experiment on six real rulebook chapters shows exactly where the old approach breaks.
  2. IIThe WallA firm cannot hire its way over the wall: more rules, more applications, more AI inside the business, and no shared map between them.
  3. IIIOne Common LanguageSix rulebook chapters from three countries, one language: how duties written in different legal traditions reduce to a single register, line by line.
  4. IVProof Before AutonomyNobody should trust automated compliance until it can prove itself: accountable people, agents that earn their tasks, evidence that runs both ways, data that stays home.
  5. VWhat We Are BuildingWhat Reg42 is building, and where we start: AI rules across the US, EU and UK at once, because adding a country should not multiply the program.
  6. VIWhat Could Prove Me WrongWhat would prove me wrong, and the signs I am watching for.

If you run compliance at a broker or a bank, come map your program with us. If you have spent a career inside a regulator, help make the standard worth trusting; it will not be trusted on my word. If you build software, the hard parts are unbuilt and the data is public. If you fund what comes next, you know where to find me.

A note on sources. Everything in these pages comes from public data, my own experiments on public rulebooks, and ten years of conversations with the people who do this work. Where a number is my estimate, it says so. Nothing here comes from any client.

And a note on the title. Don’t panic, because the wave is good news: trust is what lets a stranger hand you their savings, and proving you deserve it is about to get cheap. Regulation is cool, because rules are how a society writes down what it expects of the people it trusts, and running them well at scale is the most interesting problem I know. It took me ten years to be sure of that. I am now.

Don’t panic. The answer is 42.

Avner YoffeFounder, Reg42 · Tel Aviv, October 2026
