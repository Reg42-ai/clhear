From Tasks to the Map

Twenty-five years of adding people, and why the line can finally bend

“All information, including marketing communications, addressed by the investment firm to clients or potential clients shall be fair, clear and not misleading.” MiFID II, Article 24(3)

“A firm must ensure that a communication or a financial promotion is fair, clear and not misleading.” FCA Handbook, COBS 4.2.1R

“All member communications must be based on principles of fair dealing and good faith, must be fair and balanced.” FINRA Rule 2210(d)(1)(A)

Compliance has grown for twenty-five years by adding people. The United States employs 417,070 compliance officers, three times the number in 2000, and the wage bill for that one occupation has nearly tripled since 2010. More than $90 billion of venture and private-equity money went into regtech between 2014 and 2025, and the headcount curve did not bend, because the tools automated tasks while the map of what a firm owes stayed in people’s heads. Since 2023, machines can read a rulebook and write a policy about as well as a junior professional, at a price that fell 200-fold in two years. I ran CLHEAR’s open-source extractor over six real rulebook chapters from three countries to see what the old, word-matching approach to the map can do: it finds the UK and EU twins and not a single US one. That gap is the job. Automating the map, not the tasks, is what this wave is about.

In this piece: counting the rules; counting the people; counting the money; what the tools automated; what changed in 2023; an experiment with the map; the extrapolation.

Counting the rules

Start with the thing everyone complains about and nobody measures. The Code of Federal Regulations, the standing body of US federal rules, ran to 138,049 pages in 2000 and 194,395 in 2024, a 41% increase.1 The Federal Register, the daily gazette in which new rules, proposals and notices appear, has published between 61,584 and 107,262 pages every year of this century.2 The gazette does not shrink in quiet years; it only grows more slowly.

Page counts are a crude ruler. A page of the CFR can hold one duty or thirty, and a firm is bound by a small fraction of the whole. But the ruler points one way. Nothing in the series suggests a year in which the stock of rules fell, and the flow of new material has stayed above 60,000 pages a year for 26 years running.

The same holds outside the United States, though the measurement is worse. Neither the FCA nor EUR-Lex publishes a usable page or rule count by year, so I could not draw their lines. The experiment later in this piece gives a different kind of measure: six chapters from three rulebooks, on two narrow topics, run to 556 provisions.

Counting the people

Rules are followed by people, and the people are counted. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics has tracked an occupation called Compliance Officers since 2000. That year it counted 126,840 of them. In May 2025 it counted 417,070, earning a mean $88,400 a year.3

Two things about this chart deserve care. The occupation was redefined in 2010, which is why the bar drops between 2009 and 2010; the clean series starts there. And BLS pools three years of survey panels into each estimate, so the line is smoother than reality. Even so, from 2010 to 2025 the count grew by 4.9% a year, every year, through a financial crisis recovery, a pandemic and a deregulatory administration. If nothing changes, it passes half a million before 2030.

Multiply employment by the mean wage and you get the most conservative possible estimate of what the United States pays this one occupation: $12.7 billion in 2010 and $36.9 billion in 2025.3 That excludes benefits, contractors, consultants, lawyers, the technology, and everyone who does compliance work under a different title, which in a bank is most of the first line.

For a sense of the whole rather than one occupation, the broadest published figure is LexisNexis’s survey-based projection of what financial institutions worldwide spend on financial-crime compliance alone: $206.1 billion in the 2023 edition.12 Financial crime is one chapter of the rulebook. I do not know of a defensible number for the total, and I am suspicious of anyone who offers one.

Counting the money

Here is the puzzle. Over the same years, a great deal of capital went into fixing exactly this. KPMG’s Pulse of Fintech, using PitchBook data, counts global regtech funding at $3.9 billion in 2014, a peak of $21.3 billion in 2022, and $4.9 billion in 2025.4 Add the years up and it comes to more than $90 billion in twelve years.

Lay the funding chart over the headcount chart and the puzzle is plain. The money went in; the people kept being hired. Either the money was wasted, or it bought something other than fewer people. I think it bought something real, and I think it was the wrong thing.

What the tools automated

Compliance technology has moved in steps, and I have bought, built and sold several of them. Rules engines turned detection scenarios into alerts, and later models ranked the alerts. Screening tools matched names against sanctions lists. Reporting platforms packaged transactions for regulators. Horizon-scanning services clipped the Federal Register into daily emails.

Each step made one task faster, and each task sat inside a program that nobody had written down as a whole. What a firm owes, why it owes it, which policy, control, system and person serves each duty, and whether the evidence exists: that picture was built by people, in the specific sense that it lived in them. Lawyers read the rules, consultants mapped them, compliance teams turned the maps into policies, controls and spreadsheets. When the rules changed, they did it again. When they left, the firm hired their replacement, which is what the BLS line is counting.

The tools automated the tasks. The map stayed manual. A faster screening engine does not tell you whether the screening duty is met for the Lithuanian branch under the rules that took effect in March. So the firm hires someone to know that, and someone to check them.

What changed in 2023

Most of the map-making work is reading and writing. Read a regulation, write the obligation. Read the obligation, write the policy. Read the policy, write the control. Until recently, no machine could do any of that at the level of a junior professional. In March 2023 one could. OpenAI reported that GPT-4 scored around the 90th percentile on the Uniform Bar Exam; its predecessor, four months earlier, had scored around the 10th.6 On LegalBench, an academic suite of 162 legal reasoning tasks, GPT-4 applied rules to facts correctly 82% of the time.7

The scores have kept climbing: Harvey reports its base models moving from 60% to 90% of lawyer-quality work product between 2024 and 2026,8 and the public LegalBench leaderboard tops out near 89% today.9 I would not build a compliance program on a benchmark score. But the direction, on tests designed by lawyers to be hard for machines, is not ambiguous.

The second change is price. Epoch AI tracks the cheapest model that reaches a fixed level of capability. For GPT-4-level reading, the price went from $37.50 per million tokens in March 2023 to $0.18 in February 2025.5 For GPT-3.5-level reading, from $20 to $0.07. A million tokens is about ten times the length of the six rulebook chapters in my experiment.

The third change is that the firms are already moving, just not in compliance. Thomson Reuters surveys legal, tax, risk and government organisations every year. Those actively using generative AI went from 12% in 2024 to 22% in 2025 to 40% in 2026.10 Corporate risk departments sit near the bottom of that table. That matches what I see: the lawyers next door are using the tools, and the compliance team is still on the beach.

An experiment with the map

So far I have shown other people’s numbers. Here are mine. CLHEAR is the open standard we are building for describing compliance programs, and its first version includes a deliberately simple, fully deterministic pipeline: find the duties in a text by their modal verbs, remove near-duplicates, and match duties across jurisdictions by the share of words they have in common. No language model anywhere. I wanted to know how far that gets you, because it is a fair stand-in for how the industry has mapped rules for twenty years: keyword by keyword.11

I took six chapters on two narrow topics, client communications and best execution, from three rulebooks: the FCA Handbook (COBS 4 and COBS 11.2A), FINRA (Rules 2210 and 5310) and MiFID II (Articles 24 and 27, with Articles 44 to 47 and 64 to 66 of the delegated regulation). I read them from the regulators’ own websites on 15 October 2026 and split them into 556 provisions.

The extractor found 223 duties among the 421 binding provisions. I checked 40 of them by hand: 35 were duties, 5 were scope or deeming clauses that slipped through. Within each country, 5 were near-duplicates of another, leaving 218 distinct duties: 99 British, 59 American, 60 European.

Then the interesting part. At CLHEAR’s own strict threshold, 60% shared words, 19 of the 218 duties have a counterpart in another jurisdiction. At the loose threshold it uses for grouping, 30%, the number is 39. Every one of those matches is between the UK and the EU, and the reason is historical rather than clever: the FCA’s conduct rules were transposed from MiFID, so COBS 4.5A.9 and Article 46(5) of the delegated regulation share 93% of their words.

And the United States? Zero. Not one of the 59 American duties shares even a tenth of its words with any European or British duty. Yet the three epigraphs at the top of this piece are the same duty. FINRA says fair and balanced; Europe says fair, clear and not misleading; everyone means that you may not mislead the customer. A reader sees that in a second. A word-matcher never will, at any threshold, because the two legal traditions do not share a vocabulary.

That is the finding, and it cuts both ways. It is why twenty years of keyword tooling never produced the map: across the Atlantic, the words are different. And it is why the map is now possible: the one thing the new models are unambiguously good at is reading two differently worded sentences and saying whether they mean the same thing. The next version of this experiment, which we are running now, replaces the word-matcher with a model, keeps the clause-by-clause lineage, and puts a person’s signature on every match. The match rate is the number to watch. If it stays near 9%, I am wrong about this chapter.

The extrapolation

Put the series side by side and extend them.

  • Rules. The CFR has grown about 1.4% a year since 2000. Carried forward, it reaches about 209,000 pages in 2029. The gazette keeps publishing.
  • People. Compliance officers in the US have grown 4.9% a year since 2010. Carried forward: about 505,000 in 2029, with a wage bill well above $40 billion.
  • Machines. Epoch’s price lines fell between 9-fold and 900-fold a year depending on the task. Even at the slow end, the reading that cost $37.50 in 2023 costs cents in 2029.
  • Adoption. The professional-services usage line doubled two years running. It cannot keep doubling from 40%, but it does not need to.

The first two lines are the old world continuing, and they can continue a long time; nothing in them breaks on its own. The second two are what breaks them. When reading the rulebook costs cents and a machine can say which clauses mean the same thing, the map stops being a thing people carry around in their heads and becomes a thing a firm owns, in a file, with a lineage. Once it exists, every application, role and control in the firm can be judged by one standard: the obligation it serves. That is the shift. Earlier tools automated tasks. This time the map itself gets automated, and the headcount line, for the first time in a generation, has a reason to bend.

I have made the same projection mistake that every chart-extender makes, which is to assume the lines are independent. They are not; a bent headcount line will change what regulators ask for, and that will change the rules line. Part VI is about the ways this goes wrong. Part II is about why, even without any of this, the current model was already hitting a wall.
