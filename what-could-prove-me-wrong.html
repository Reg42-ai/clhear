What Could Prove Me Wrong

The risks, and the signs I’m watching for

I’m confident about the direction. I’m much less sure about the pace and the path. These are the risks I take most seriously, what I would expect to see first if each one is real, and how we are building for it.

Firms may prefer separate tools to one system

We would see it first in design partners buying single applications and leaving the map aside. The common language also works between separate applications, which is one reason CLHEAR is open.

Regulators may not accept a machine-drawn map

The early sign would be regulators questioning obligations that no person wrote. Every obligation links back to the exact wording it came from, and accountable people sign off.

AI may not be accurate enough for a task

Our own tests would show it first, with the machine doing worse than the people it is meant to help. In that case the task stays with people until the machine does better.

A young standard may not earn trust

If practitioners stay away from CLHEAR, we will know. That is why we are developing it in the open, with practitioners as contributors and reviewers.

Established platforms may add mapping as a feature

Watch for obligation mapping showing up inside existing compliance suites. Our answer is an open standard that no single application owns, which those suites can use too.

Rules may move faster than the map

This one would show up as the map falling behind regulatory change. The map updates continuously from the source texts, clause by clause, and the EU’s 2026 change to its own AI Act dates shows why that matters.

None of these, I believe, removes the need. Each one changes the path, and the order in which we build. That is why the first products are designed to be useful on their own.
